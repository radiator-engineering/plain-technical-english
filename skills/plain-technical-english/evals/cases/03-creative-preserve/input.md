The tide went out slow that year, and slower every year after, as if the sea had grown tired of the town and was leaving it the way you leave a marriage — in inches, politely, while everyone pretends not to notice. Marisol counted the exposed pilings from the cannery window: eleven, then thirteen, then, by August, seventeen, each one furred green and leaning like a drunk apostle.

When the letter arrived, her heart skipped a beat and her blood ran cold. It goes without saying that letters from the diocese never carried good news, and this one was no exception to the rule, being, at the end of the day, the straw that broke the camel's back.

She read it twice on the loading dock, in the vinegar smell of the brine vats, and then she folded it into the shape her mother had taught her — a boat, of course, always a boat — and set it in the retreating water, where it sat stubbornly on the mud, going nowhere, like everything else she had ever tried to send away.
